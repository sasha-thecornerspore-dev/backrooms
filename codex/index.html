§ 01 — the tether · DOC-GEO-01

Your county is a world.

The Backrooms are not somewhere else. They are here, one layer down, and the maze knows exactly where your body is standing when you fall in.

Every descent is tethered to real ground. The seam you drop through opens beneath the actual place you were — so the walls you walk are drawn from the county you are in, and the door nearest you in the real world is always the deepest one available to you in the maze. Presence is the whole physics of it. Walk to a real place and the layer under it remembers you deeply; reach for a far one from your chair and what you leave behind is faint. Distance costs. Nearness is free.

This is why the way in asks where you are, and keeps asking. It is not tracking you for anyone's benefit but the map's — it needs to know where the doors are, because the doors are real coordinates and the atlas is the registry of every one that has surfaced. The game hands the same instrument that has always sorted people by their ground back to you: a map, and the standing to read it.

§ 02 — the one real door

Of everything a renderer could point at, it was built to tell the truth about this block.

Every version of the Backrooms begins at one real address. Not a set. Not a render of somewhere. A door you could stand in front of. The engine that draws the maze fakes every surface, because faking surfaces is the only thing a raycaster can do. And the 800 block of North Carey is also made of faked surfaces — but those were faked by hand, over sixty years, by the city itself.

The medium and the subject are the same technique. Of everything this renderer could have pointed at, the one subject it was accidentally built to tell the truth about is a block that was made out of illusions.

Four instruments did the work. Each one is a piece of paper. Each one is still on file. Read in order, they are not four events — they are one continuous sentence, and it has never reached a period.

§ 03 — the map
1937

A federal map rings the block in red and grades the ground hazardous.

The Home Owners' Loan Corporation — a New Deal agency — drew Residential Security Maps of American cities to tell lenders where mortgages were safe. Baltimore's map sorts every neighborhood A through D and colors it: green for "best," down to red for "hazardous," grade D. Black West Baltimore is ringed in red.

Not one building was inspected to earn that grade. The hazard the surveyors scored was the people — the appraisal forms recorded the racial makeup of a neighborhood as a defect of the land itself, an "infiltration" that lowered the value of the ground. That is the lie the whole game is built to indict: not decay, not danger, but a government instrument ruling that human beings made land worth less by living on it. Every later paper on this block only enforces the red line drawn here.

§ 04 — the plan
1961

Urban renewal clears the inside of the block and leaves parks no one asked for.

A generation after the red line, the city came back with a plan. In Harlem Park, "urban renewal" meant hollowing out the interiors of the residential blocks — demolishing the alley houses and back structures crowded inside each square of rowhouses — to open the middle into fenced green inner-block parks. The plan, adopted in 1961, envisioned twenty-nine of them.

The residents did not ask for the parks. The city did not fully intend to keep them. Cut off from the street, hard to see and harder to maintain, many became exactly what you leave in a place you have already decided to write off. The plan that promised to open the block up is the plan that hollowed it out — and the hollow it left is, sixty years later, the only unsealed way in.

§ 05 — the notice
2004

Notice 30150A. The file is opened. It is never closed.

In 2004 a vacant-building notice — 30150A — is issued against 806 N Carey. It advises that the structure is "condemned as being unsafe or dangerous for occupancy or use," and that "the public is warned to keep away." A front door made of paper closes over a front door made of concrete block.

And then nothing resolves. The notice is never appealed, never demolished, never lifted. Its status is EXTENSION — extended, and extended, and extended again, for more than twenty years. It is the most Backrooms word in the whole dataset. A file that is never closed is a room you can never leave. That is the door you fall through.

§ 06 — the fourth instrument
the map came back

First they drew the line to keep the money out. Then they used the same line to aim money designed to fail.

For fifty years redlining meant starvation: the red district on the 1937 map was where loans would not go, so the block was denied the ordinary mortgage credit that lets people repair and hold a home. Then, in the 2000s, the instrument inverted. The lending finally arrived in West Baltimore — but it arrived as reverse redlining: predatory subprime mortgages steered into the exact neighborhoods the old map had condemned, aimed at Black borrowers, structured to fail. The same red outline. First a wall against money, then a funnel for it. Both times, dispossession.

The City of Baltimore sued Wells Fargo in 2008, alleging the bank targeted Black neighborhoods with high-cost subprime loans that ended in a wave of foreclosures the city then had to absorb. Former loan officers swore affidavits in the case describing, in their own accounts, the language the trade allegedly used inside its own offices for those loans and the borrowers it aimed them at — language ugly enough that it reached the national papers. Four years later, in 2012, the U.S. Department of Justice reached a separate $175 million settlement with the bank over the same pattern of discriminatory lending against Black and Hispanic borrowers — then one of the largest fair-lending settlements in the department's history. The bank admitted no wrongdoing. Baltimore's own case was resolved around the same time.

And the machine did not stop being careless once the lawsuits ended. Years later the same bank disclosed that a software error in the system that decided who could keep their home had wrongly denied families the mortgage modification that would have saved them — and had foreclosed on many of them anyway. A calculation. A glitch. A house. The instrument that starves a place and the instrument that drowns it turn out to be the same instrument, and it is still running.

This chapter is grounded in the documented public record — court filings, the U.S. Department of Justice, and contemporaneous reporting. Every claim above is sourced in where the record lives.

§ 07 — the skin

The block wears its condition as illusion. That is the exact thing a raycaster does.

Stand on the row and almost nothing you see is what it claims to be. The stone fronts are Formstone — a stucco troweled and tinted to imitate cut masonry, patented in Baltimore in 1937 and slathered across the city's rowhouses. John Waters called it "the polyester of brick." A stone wall that is not stone.

And some of the boarded windows are not boards. To make a written-off block read as lived-in from a passing car, the city and private owners have spent decades dressing the vacants to look occupied — painting scenes onto the plywood, hanging curtains inside boarded frames, and, in one Baltimore effort, gluing life-sized photographs of windows over the boards. A resident quoted at the time said it plainest: "even though it's fake, you have to look up on it to see that it's fake." That is, word for word, what a raycaster does to you — a flat surface, painted to read as depth, that only gives itself away if you get close enough to test it.

A false-stone wall over a real one. A photograph of a window where a window used to be. The block was not abandoned. It was processed — and dressed, afterward, to look occupied.

§ 08 — the wanderers

Someone was here before you, and left the walls talking.

The record is not only paper the state filed. It is also paper people left — the scraps you find scratched and taped through the halls, out of order, in the hand of a wanderer who signs only m. Down here they read like the last layer of the same story: a person processed by the same instruments, telling you what the files never will.

Find them in the maze and you steady, a little — proof you are not the only one who was ever here. Read enough of m.'s thread and you will start to suspect m. never found the way back up. Read all of it and you will know why the field manual exists.

§ 09 — the arithmetic · the turn

The grain is not settling like an old house. Someone is spending it.

This is the page m. worked out three times because they did not want it to be right. It is the page that turns a haunted rowhouse into a countdown.

Recall the doctrine: the Planck grain — the resolution of reality itself — ran wide in the layers above, wide enough to spin up wildly different universes for almost nothing, and here near the floor it is nearly closed. The easy reading is that it is old, and old things settle. The easy reading is wrong. Excavation was never free. Every world ever dug cost grain from the layer beneath it, and we are the deepest cut — the fine print at the bottom of the stack, with nothing below us left to mine. So the only thing still drawing the reserve down toward zero is us. Every careless drop-in from a couch a thousand miles away mints a faint copy of a person out of the last grain there is. The clock is not weather. The clock is the crowd.

And it is a clock you can read. Field Recovery never had a map of the maze — they had the beacon, the one signal that costs no grain and reaches out instead of pulling presence in. Fire it on a schedule and its return timing drifts by a hair each week as the grain tightens around it. Plot the drift, fit the curve, and the curve names a date: the day the grain welds shut and every seam between inside and outside welds with it. Case ∅ is not the investigation of a haunted address. It is arithmetic somebody did and could not un-know.

The thing doing the foreclosing is not a monster. It is a System — an indifferent clerk the size of physics, the process that administers a reality too old to be created anymore. It does not chase. It itemizes. It reads a ledger in the walls and waits to reach your line. The grain closing is simply an old form left to run its course — the same municipal machinery that quietly foreclosed one real city block, scaled up to the render itself. It has no malice because it has no attention. And it has exactly one blind spot, which is the entire reason there is anything left to do.

Case ∅ carries no serial. ∅ is not a number. ∅ is a status — and if you decoded the rune line and found your way here, it is quietly, already, yours.

Read that way, m.'s last two findings stop being comfort and become measurement. "Further in is not always worse" — because finer grain is more real, not less; the deepest layer is the most detailed, most fixed, most true place in existence. And "out.", with its little arrow, was never a plea to climb stairs that do not exist. Out is through the floor, past the last grain, into whatever the excavation was always digging toward.

§ 10 — the counter-claim

The one thing a ledger cannot foreclose is a claim someone is still making.

The System can foreclose an absence. A status left to lapse. A body that only ever dropped in from far away and was never really there. But its forms have no field for presence, and a person genuinely, deeply here — both feet, whole weight — is the one claim it cannot deny in absentia. That is not a loophole. It is the only physics left that runs in our favour.

So the move is not to escape. There is no up. The move is to assert. Two acts, and the whole arc turns on them being the same act:

walk in — do not drop in

Go on foot. Body committed to the coordinate, presence deep and real — the substrate spends nothing, and remembers you deeply. Drop in from afar and you mint a thin copy from the last grain there is. Nearness is free. Distance is a withdrawal.

fire the beacon as a claim

Not an SOS. A counter-claim, filed outward along the wire: i was here. put it in the file. Notice 30150A's EXTENSION — never closed — is the whole move in miniature: the one status the System never foreclosed, the door left ajar.

And here is the image the whole record resolves on. A single beacon looks like a solitary lifeline. Pointed at every other marked channel, it is a network — the marked counting themselves. The individual SOS was always a roll call of the living. Silence finalizes ∅. Answered — kept answering — the roll call is the one thing holding the seams open.

Nothing reopens the grain. That clock never stops. But a claim made, and made again, is an extension that for once stays an extension.

You can do exactly this inside the maze. Descend to the deep stacks, find the radio, and let it read you the ledger. Then walk — do not drop — to a presence, register your beacon to the one status that never closed, and file your claim. The next section is how the files open for you.
